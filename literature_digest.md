# Literature Digest — Central Bank Digital Currencies and Financial Stability

## Overview

This digest distils the SSRN working-paper evidence on whether retail central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) threaten financial stability, organised around the five questions into which the debate has crystallised: bank deposit disintermediation, run risk, monetary-policy transmission, cross-border and multi-CBDC platforms, and adoption evidence. Three findings stand out. First, the disintermediation feared early in the literature looks quantitatively modest in both models and live data: a marginal dollar of CBDC replaces roughly 80 cents of bank deposits, yet banks recoup much of the lost funding in wholesale markets so lending falls far less (Whited, Wu & Xiao; Di Maggio et al. on India's digital rupee). Second, the sharper threat is run risk: because CBDC is a risk-free digital claim, it dominates cash as a flight-to-safety asset, which is why design tools — holding limits, tiered remuneration, and convertibility restrictions — have moved to the centre of the policy discussion (Venturi; Lee & Lee's calibration from realized stablecoin runs; Lambert et al.'s holding-limit estimates). Third, the cross-border and adoption literatures point in the same pragmatic direction: multi-CBDC platforms such as Project mBridge promise efficiency gains but raise geopolitical fragmentation questions, while actual take-up of live retail CBDCs (the e-CNY pilots, the Bahamian SandDollar, JAM-DEX) remains modest — which itself blunts, but does not eliminate, the stability risks.

## Kept papers (15 distinct papers)

### A. Bank deposit disintermediation

1. **Will Central Bank Digital Currency Disintermediate Banks?** — Toni M. Whited, Yufeng Wu, Kairong Xiao — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4112644) — A marginal dollar of CBDC displaces about 80 cents of bank deposits, but bank lending falls by only about one-fourth as much because banks substitute wholesale funding, leaving them more exposed to interest-rate risk.

2. **Central Bank Digital Currency and Bank Intermediation** — Ramón Adalid, Álvaro Álvarez-Blázquez, Katrin Assenmacher, Lorenzo Burlon, et al. (ECB Occasional Paper No. 293) — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4108346) — Assesses the effects of a digital euro on euro-area bank intermediation through complementary approaches and concludes that an appropriately designed CBDC need not destabilise the banking system.

3. **Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC), Bank Deposits, and Digital Payments: First Evidence from a Live Retail CBDC** — Marco Di Maggio, Pulak Ghosh, Soumya Kanti Ghosh, Nishant Vats, Andrew Wu — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4779520) — Using India's digital-rupee rollout, a non-interest-bearing, capped retail CBDC generates sizable changes in deposits and digital payments but only a transitory effect on bank lending.

### B. Run risk and financial stability

4. **Central Bank Digital Currency: Financial Fragility and Bank Runs** — G. C. Venturi — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4463929) — An interest-bearing CBDC raises run risk on the banking sector, although once a run is under way asset prices fall far less than they would without CBDC, so private banks bear smaller losses.

5. **Bounding a Digital Run: Realized Stablecoin Runs and CBDC Holding Limits for the Digital Euro** — Jaewook Lee, Seungju Lee — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7411829) — Recovers a flight schedule from realized stablecoin runs (53% of holdings on the peak day, 84% within two days) and uses it to calibrate digital-euro holding limits that bound a CBDC-driven digital run.

6. **CBDCs, Banknotes and Bank Deposits: The Financial Stability Nexus** — José Ramón Martínez Resano (Banco de España) — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5474966) — Maps the financial-stability nexus of a monetary ecosystem extended to include a CBDC and argues that a calibrated "tiering" of money preserves stability across banknotes, deposits and CBDC.

### C. Monetary policy transmission

7. **The Case for Convenience: How CBDC Design Choices Impact Monetary Policy Pass-Through** — Rodney J. Garratt, Jiaheng Yu, Haoxiang Zhu — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4004341) — A CBDC that adds payment convenience (and possibly interest) puts competitive pressure on banks' deposit rates and can strengthen the pass-through of policy-rate changes, though remuneration may reshape market composition.

8. **CBDC and Monetary Policy Transmission: Why Household Heterogeneity Matters** — Sargam Gupta, Ankita Mandal — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7582219) — Household heterogeneity makes CBDC's impact on monetary-policy transmission differ systematically between advanced economies and emerging-market and developing economies.

### D. Cross-border payments and multi-CBDC platforms

9. **Central Bank Digital Currencies and International Payments** — Charles M. Kahn, Chao He (SWIFT Institute Working Paper No. 2020-2) — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4112382) — Interview-based documentation (22 senior payments professionals) of today's cross-border payment processes and of how direct multi-CBDC (mCBDC) exchanges could replace correspondent-banking chains.

10. **Can Central Bank Digital Currencies Replace SWIFT for Cross-Border Payments?** — A. Baker — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5186429) — Multi-CBDC initiatives such as Project mBridge could complement or displace SWIFT-based correspondent settlement, reducing reliance on USD-based settlement in future cross-border payment ecosystems.

11. **Global Financial Order at a Crossroads: Do CBDCs Lead to Balkanisation or Harmonization?** — Cheng-Yun (CY) Tsang, Ping-Kuei Chen — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4636943) — Counteracting regulatory and geopolitical forces determine whether rising CBDCs fragment ("balkanise") or harmonise the global financial order.

### E. Adoption evidence

12. **Adoption of Central Bank Digital Currencies: Initial Evidence from China** — HaiChen Bai, Lin William Cong, Mei Luo, Ping Xie — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5022129) — The earliest and most comprehensive empirical documentation of e-CNY adoption after the People's Bank of China promoted pilots in selected regions using both regulatory power and economic incentives.

13. **Retail CBDCs in practice: the experience of the SandDollar, e-CNY and JAM-DEX** — Sharon Branch, Yvonne Cooper, Cecilia Franco, Jon Frost, et al. — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5398695) — Takes stock of the world's three live retail CBDCs and what their design and usage experience teaches central banks contemplating issuance.

14. **CBDC Lessons from the Caribbean: Analyzing Central Bank Digital Currencies in Jamaica and The Bahamas** — N. Anthony — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5390545) — The CBDCs of both Jamaica and The Bahamas have so far failed to gain traction with either consumers or businesses, cautioning against assuming retail CBDC demand.

15. **Digital Euro Demand: Design, Individuals' Payment Preferences and Socioeconomic Factors** — Claudia Lambert, Chloe Larkou, Cosimo Pancaro, Antonella Pellicani, Meri Sintonen (ECB Working Paper No. 2980) — [SSRN working paper](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4958524) — Structural demand estimation on euro-area consumer data: unconstrained digital-euro holdings could reach 3–28% of household liquid assets (€0.12–1.11 trillion), but an illustrative €3,000 holding limit caps take-up at 2–9% (€0.10–0.38 trillion).

## Key table

| | Physical money | Digital money |
|---|---|---|
| **Legal tender (or the nearest equivalent)** | Banknotes and coins | CBDCs |
| **Regulated to a lower standard, not legal tender** | nil (some IOUs such as German "Notgeld" come close) | Accounts at AFIs, a Visa-card, PayPal, Alipay, LHV's Cuber etc. |
| **Unregulated** | Commodity money such as gold coins; local coupons; etc. | Cryptocurrencies like Bitcoin, Ethereum, etc. |

*Caption: Table 1, "The different types of competing money" (p. 3), from Kilian Wenker, "Retail Central Bank Digital Currencies, Disintermediation and Financial Privacy: The Case of the Bahamian Sand Dollar" ([arXiv:2204.01535](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.01535)). The SSRN PDFs of the kept papers are gated, so this table is extracted from the publicly accessible arXiv preprint of a closely related paper on the same question — a scenario analysis of the commercial-bank funding, disintermediation and run risks of a live retail CBDC (the Bahamian Sand Dollar), the same CBDC examined by the kept paper "Retail CBDCs in practice" (no. 13 above). Per the source: the table excludes physical and electronic play money, and "Accounts at AFIs" can include, for example, time deposits at a commercial bank, reserves at a central bank, or balances at a payment service provider (PSP).*

## De-duplication note

69 distinct candidate papers were gathered from SSRN searches across the five angles (many surfaced repeatedly under different phrasings). The 30 strongest candidates were carried into the near-duplicate removal step. **15 near-duplicates were dropped** — the same paper appearing under a different title or landing page, or different papers answering the same question with the same method — leaving the **15 genuinely distinct papers** listed above as the basis for this digest. Note: SSRN landing pages are protected by bot verification, so the metadata and one-line contributions of the kept papers were verified against their public versions hosted by the NBER, the ECB, the Federal Reserve, RePEc/IDEAS, the IHS and arXiv.
